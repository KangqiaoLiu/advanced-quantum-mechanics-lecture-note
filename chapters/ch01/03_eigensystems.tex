\section{Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors of Operators (Matrices)}
\subsection*{1) Eigenvalues and eigenstates of a general operator}
The eigenvalue equation of an operator is
\begin{equation}
\op A\ket a=\lambda\ket a,
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ is called an eigenvalue of $\op A$ and $\ket a$ is called an eigenvector of $\op A$.

Use the completeness relation to derive the component equation. Write
$\ket a=\sum_n a_n\ket n$ and multiply $\op A\ket a=\lambda\ket a$
by $\bra m$:
\begin{equation}
\mel m{\op A}a=\sum_n a_n\mel m{\op A}n
=\lambda\sum_n a_n\braket m n=\lambda a_m.
\end{equation}
Thus $\sum_n A_{mn}a_n=\lambda a_m$ for each $m$, or,
\begin{equation}
Aa=\lambda a,\qquad (A-\lambda\mathbf1)a=0.
\end{equation}
The condition for a nonzero eigenvector $a\ne0$ is
\begin{equation}
\det(A-\lambda\mathbf1)=0.
\end{equation}
This is the secular equation in an $N$-dimensional space:
\begin{equation}
\begin{vmatrix}
A_{11}-\lambda&A_{12}&\cdots&A_{1N}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}-\lambda&\cdots&A_{2N}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
A_{N1}&A_{N2}&\cdots&A_{NN}-\lambda
\end{vmatrix}=0.
\end{equation}
One thereby obtains $N$ eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ ($i=1,2,\ldots,N$). Substituting each into
\begin{equation}
(A-\lambda_i\mathbf1)a=0
\end{equation}
gives the corresponding eigenvector $a$.

\textbf{Example.} Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
\begin{equation}
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The secular equation is
\begin{equation}
\begin{vmatrix}1-\lambda&0\\0&-1-\lambda\end{vmatrix}=0,
\quad\text{i.e.,}\quad \lambda^2-1=0,
\end{equation}
and the eigenvalues are $\lambda_\pm=\pm1$.

Let $a=(a_1,a_2)^{\mathsf T}$. For $\lambda=1$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&-2\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}a_1\\a_2\end{pmatrix}=0.
\end{equation}
The normalized eigenvector is $a=(1,0)^{\mathsf T}$. Similarly, taking $\lambda=-1$ gives $a=(0,1)^{\mathsf T}$.
\subsection*{2) Hermitian operators}
If $\op A^\dagger=\op A$, or $A^\dagger=A$, the operator $\op A$ is called Hermitian and $A$ is called a Hermitian matrix.

We discuss the following properties of Hermitian operators.
\paragraph{a) Conjugate symmetry.}
\begin{equation}
\mel a{\op A}b=\bigl(\mel b{\op A^\dagger}a\bigr)^*=\bigl(\mel b{\op A}a\bigr)^*.
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}
\mel a{\op A}a=\bigl(\mel a{\op A}a\bigr)^*,
\end{equation}
which shows that the expectation value $\mel a{\op A}a$ of a Hermitian operator is real.

For an anti-Hermitian operator, $\op A^\dagger=-\op A$ and
\begin{equation}
\mel a{\op A}a=-\bigl(\mel a{\op A}a\bigr)^*,
\end{equation}
so its expectation value is purely imaginary.
\paragraph{b) Reality of eigenvalues and orthogonality.}
Suppose
\begin{equation}
\op A\ket i=\lambda_i\ket i,\qquad \op A\ket j=\lambda_j\ket j.
\end{equation}
From
\begin{equation}
\mel j{\op A}i=\lambda_i\braket j i
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\bra j\op A=\bra j\lambda_j^*,\qquad
\mel j{\op A}i=\lambda_j^*\braket j i,
\end{equation}
we obtain
\begin{equation}
(\lambda_i-\lambda_j^*)\braket j i=0.
\end{equation}
For $j=i$ and $\braket i i\ne0$, $\lambda_i=\lambda_i^*$: the eigenvalues of a Hermitian operator are real.

For $j\ne i$, if $\lambda_i-\lambda_j\ne0$, then $\braket j i=0$. Thus eigenstates corresponding to different eigenvalues of a Hermitian operator are orthogonal. Since they can always be normalized, the orthonormality condition is
\begin{equation}
\braket i j=\delta_{ij}.
\end{equation}
\textbf{Question:} Are eigenstates corresponding to the same eigenvalue orthogonal?
\paragraph{c) Orthogonalization by linear superposition (Schmidt orthogonalization).}
Suppose several eigenstates correspond to the same eigenvalue, i.e., there is degeneracy. For example, for a $g$-fold degenerate eigenvalue ($g\ge2$),
\begin{equation}
\op A\ket{i,j}=\lambda_i\ket{i,j},\qquad j=1,\ldots,g.
\end{equation}
Are these $g$ eigenvectors orthogonal?

Redefine $g$ new states:
\begin{equation}
\ket{i,n}=\sum_{j=1}^g C_{nj}\ket{i,j},\qquad n=1,2,\ldots,g.
\end{equation}
Since
\begin{equation}
\op A\ket{i,n}=\sum_{j=1}^g C_{nj}\op A\ket{i,j}
=\lambda_i\sum_{j=1}^g C_{nj}\ket{i,j}=\lambda_i\ket{i,n},
\end{equation}
each $\ket{i,n}$ is still an eigenstate of $\op A$ with eigenvalue $\lambda_i$. Hence it suffices to show that the $\ket{i,n}$ can be made orthonormal.

Here is an explicit construction of the required vectors. Start with
linearly independent eigenvectors $\ket{v_1},\dots,\ket{v_g}$ in the
degenerate eigenspace, and normalize the first:
\begin{equation}
\ket{e_1}=\frac{\ket{v_1}}{\sqrt{\braket{v_1}{v_1}}}.
\end{equation}
Remove the component of the second vector parallel to $\ket{e_1}$:
\begin{equation}
\ket{w_2}=\ket{v_2}-\ket{e_1}\braket{e_1}{v_2},
\quad
\braket{e_1}{w_2}=\braket{e_1}{v_2}
-\braket{e_1}{e_1}\braket{e_1}{v_2}=0.
\end{equation}
The original pair is independent, so $\ket{w_2}\ne0$.
Set $\ket{e_2}=\ket{w_2}/\sqrt{\braket{w_2}{w_2}}$.
For each later vector, repeat:
\begin{equation}
\ket{w_n}=\ket{v_n}-\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}
 \ket{e_k}\braket{e_k}{v_n},
\qquad
\ket{e_n}=\frac{\ket{w_n}}{\sqrt{\braket{w_n}{w_n}}}.
\end{equation}
For $j<n$,
\begin{equation}
\braket{e_j}{w_n}=\braket{e_j}{v_n}
-\sum_{k<n}\delta_{jk}\braket{e_k}{v_n}=0.
\end{equation}
Since all $\ket{v_n}$ have the same eigenvalue $\lambda_i$,
every linear combination $\ket{w_n}$ also satisfies
$\op A\ket{w_n}=\lambda_i\ket{w_n}$.
This is the Gram--Schmidt construction \emph{inside the eigenspace}.
It yields orthonormal eigenvectors, including in the degenerate case:
\begin{equation}
\braket{i,m}{j,n}=\delta_{ij}\delta_{mn}.
\end{equation}
Here distinct eigenvalues supply the factor $\delta_{ij}$,
and Gram--Schmidt supplies $\delta_{mn}$.

\textbf{Conclusion:} Eigenstates of a Hermitian operator can be chosen orthonormal, whether the eigenvalues are degenerate or nondegenerate.
\paragraph{d) Completeness.}
In a finite-dimensional space we can establish completeness by induction.
A Hermitian $N\times N$ matrix has an eigenvalue by the fundamental
theorem of algebra applied to its characteristic polynomial, and
the reality argument above makes that eigenvalue real.
Choose a normalized eigenvector $\ket{e_1}$.
Its orthogonal complement is invariant: whenever
$\braket{e_1}{v}=0$,
\begin{equation}
\braket{e_1}{\op A v}
=\mel{e_1}{\op A}v
=\lambda_1\braket{e_1}{v}=0.
\end{equation}
Restrict $\op A$ to this $(N-1)$-dimensional invariant subspace.
The restriction remains Hermitian. Iterating produces $N$ mutually
orthonormal eigenvectors, including any necessary orthogonalization
within degenerate subspaces.
Every ket can therefore be expanded as
\begin{equation}
\ket\psi=\sum_{n=1}^N\ket{e_n}\braket{e_n}{\psi}
=\left(\sum_{n=1}^N\ket{e_n}\bra{e_n}\right)\ket\psi.
\end{equation}
Because $\ket\psi$ is arbitrary,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\sum_{n=1}^N\ket{e_n}\bra{e_n}=\mathbf1.}
\end{equation}
This is the finite-dimensional spectral theorem. Additional
domain and continuous-spectrum considerations arise in infinite
dimension.
