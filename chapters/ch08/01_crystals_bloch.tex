An ordinary insulator has an energy gap that prevents electrons from moving freely through its interior. Yet some insulating materials possess conducting states at their edges or surfaces. A striking example comes from the quantum Hall effect: for electrons confined approximately to two dimensions in a strong magnetic field, the transverse electrical conductance can form remarkably precise plateaus at integer multiples of $e^2/h$. The precision of these plateaus survives moderate changes in the sample and experimental conditions. Such observations suggest that certain properties of quantum states are controlled by something more global than the energy of an electron at a particular point.

To see what that global structure means, imagine following an electron state while a parameter changes continuously and eventually returns to its starting value. The state may turn or acquire a phase along the way. A closed curve can wind around a point, and a surface can wrap around a sphere; these whole-path properties are familiar ideas from topology. In a crystal, the relevant parameter is often the electron's \emph{crystal momentum}, and the family of quantum eigenstates across all momenta can have a nontrivial geometric structure. Topological invariants assign discrete values to aspects of this structure. As long as the bulk energy gap stays open and the symmetries needed for the invariant are maintained, the value cannot change continuously.

This leads to an important connection between the interior and the boundary of a material. Two insulating systems can have different topological invariants even when both possess an energy gap. Joining them can produce states localized near their interface; in a two-dimensional quantum Hall system, the corresponding boundary channels carry current. The connection between the topology of the bulk and the possible boundary states is called \emph{bulk--boundary correspondence}~\cite{TKNN1982,QiZhang2011}. It provides a way to understand transport and boundary phenomena from the quantum states inside a material.

We will build this picture using a chain of sites connected by alternating hopping strengths. Depending on the pattern of strong and weak connections, an open chain can have localized states near its ends. The \emph{Su--Schrieffer--Heeger} (SSH) model makes the relation between this boundary behavior, a winding number, and a Berry phase accessible through two-component quantum states. We then extend the same geometric ideas to two-dimensional bands and their quantized Hall response. Along the way, the Berry phase developed in the quantum-dynamics chapter becomes a practical tool for describing matter.

\section{From a Periodic Chain to Energy Bands}
A crystal contains a very large number of repeating microscopic units. Its atomic spacing provides a natural length scale, and the periodicity allows us to describe one-particle states using a wavevector $k$. We first construct this description for a simple chain, then use the resulting energy bands and eigenstates to examine topology.

\subsection*{1) A tight-binding chain}
Consider one localized orbital $\ket n$ in each unit cell $n$ of a one-dimensional lattice, with lattice spacing $a$. The states are orthonormal, $\braket m n=\delta_{mn}$. An electron can move between neighboring sites by the \emph{hopping amplitude} $t$:
\begin{equation}
 \op H=-t\sum_n\bigl(\ket{n+1}\bra n+\ket n\bra{n+1}\bigr).
\end{equation}
With periodic boundary conditions, $\ket{n+N}=\ket n$. Define the normalized Bloch states
\begin{equation}
\ket k=\frac1{\sqrt N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{\ii kna}\ket n,
\qquad k=\frac{2\pi m}{Na},\ m=0,\ldots,N-1.
\end{equation}
Translation by one cell multiplies $\ket k$ by $e^{-\ii ka}$, and the Hamiltonian acts diagonally:
\begin{equation}
 \op H\ket k=-2t\cos(ka)\ket k.
\end{equation}
The dispersion $E(k)=-2t\cos(ka)$ repeats under $k\mapsto k+2\pi/a$. The interval $[-\pi/a,\pi/a)$ is one \emph{Brillouin zone}. A band is the energy of an eigenstate of the periodic Hamiltonian as a function of crystal momentum.

\subsection*{2) More than one orbital per unit cell}
Suppose a cell has two orbitals, $A$ and $B$. The Bloch wavefunction has a two-component coefficient $\ket{u(k)}=(u_A(k),u_B(k))^{\mathsf T}$, and its energies follow from a $2\times2$ Hermitian \emph{Bloch Hamiltonian}:
\begin{equation}
 \op h(k)\ket{u_n(k)}=E_n(k)\ket{u_n(k)},\qquad n=1,2.
\end{equation}
Any $2\times2$ Hermitian matrix can be written
\begin{equation}
 \op h(k)=d_0(k)\mathbf1_2+\vect d(k)\cdot\vect\sigma,\qquad
 E_\pm(k)=d_0(k)\pm|\vect d(k)|.
\end{equation}
The bands are separated by a gap whenever $\vect d(k)\ne0$. At fixed particle filling, a band insulator results if all occupied bands are separated from the empty bands by a finite gap. These are single-particle bands: interactions may require a more general many-body description.

\subsection*{3) The role of geometry}
Besides its energy, a Bloch eigenstate has a direction in the internal space of orbital amplitudes. A smoothly evolving normalized eigenvector $\ket{u_n(k)}$ is defined only up to a $k$-dependent phase:
$\ket{u_n(k)}\mapsto e^{\ii\chi(k)}\ket{u_n(k)}$.
Exactly this freedom appeared in the Berry-phase discussion. As $k$ traverses the Brillouin zone, the eigenvector can accumulate a geometric phase. Certain quantized properties of the eigenvectors remain unchanged when the Hamiltonian is smoothly deformed without closing the gap and without breaking the symmetry that protects the invariant. Such properties are the starting point of topological band theory~\cite{Asboth2016,Zak1989}.
