What changes when two identical particles are exchanged?

Combine overlapping one-particle Gaussian orbitals into symmetric and antisymmetric two-particle wavefunctions. Compare bosonic bunching and fermionic exclusion in full position-space densities.

Self-paced physical investigationIdentical-particle exchangeTwo-particle probabilityCheckable mathematics

Motivation

How does indistinguishability reshape a joint position measurement?

For identical particles, the two labelings of a pair of occupied orbitals describe the same physical arrangement. The total wavefunction is constrained to be symmetric for bosons or antisymmetric for fermions under exchange of both coordinates. Spatially overlapping orbitals generate interference between the two assignments, so the joint probability density differs from a simple product. The antisymmetric wavefunction must vanish when both particles are measured at the same position for a spinless two-fermion state. The effect can be explored quantitatively by changing the separation and width of two normalized orbitals.

Predict first. Bring the two orbital centers closer together and compare joint densities for the symmetric and antisymmetric wavefunctions. What happens along x₁=x₂? What happens to the normalized fermionic state when the two orbitals become linearly dependent?

Prerequisites: Identical particles, product states, exchange operators, Gaussian integrals and squared wavefunction amplitudes.

Derive the result

Normalize exchanged orbital assignments

Let φa(x),φb(x) be individually normalized real Gaussian orbitals with centers at −d/2 and +d/2, both having probability-density variance σ². Their scalar overlap is S=〈φa|φb〉=exp[−d²/(8σ²)]. The two distinguishable labelings have amplitudes φa(x₁)φb(x₂) and φb(x₁)φa(x₂).

Ψ±(x₁,x₂)=[φa(x₁)φb(x₂)±φb(x₁)φa(x₂)]/√[2(1±S²)].

The normalization depends on S because these assignment amplitudes are not orthogonal when the orbitals overlap. Under coordinate exchange x₁↔x₂, the bosonic amplitude Ψ+ is unchanged and the fermionic Ψ− acquires a minus sign. Their squared densities both respect coordinate exchange, but have different interference terms.

For x₁=x₂=x the antisymmetric numerator cancels exactly, so Ψ−(x,x)=0. If the orbitals become identical, S→1 and the antisymmetric numerator vanishes everywhere, expressing the impossibility of placing two identical spinless fermions into the same spatial orbital. Symmetric orbital occupations remain allowed and can be enhanced by overlap.

Exactly soluble limits and sanity checks

At large separation d≫σ the overlap S approaches zero and normalization tends to 1/√2 for either exchange symmetry. At d→0, the symmetric two-particle state becomes φ(x₁)φ(x₂), whereas the normalized antisymmetric construction is undefined at exact orbital coincidence. The slider stops at positive d to avoid assigning a spurious finite value to a Pauli-forbidden state.

What assumptions define this model?

Two identical spinless noninteracting particles described by real normalized Gaussian orbital wavefunctions in one spatial dimension. The model concerns a prepared two-orbital state rather than a thermal many-body ensemble; spin, interactions, scattering phases and wave-packet time evolution are outside the scope.

Self-directed physics exercises

Three independent investigations

  1. Identify symmetry without particle labels. Choose moderate separation and compare densities at (x₁,x₂) and (x₂,x₁). Verify equal probabilities for either statistics even though the fermionic amplitude changes sign.
  2. Investigate the diagonal. Move the coincidence position x and compare bosonic and fermionic diagonal densities. Explain the node of the antisymmetric wavefunction and the role of orbital overlap.
  3. Vary orbital overlap. Reduce d/σ. Track S and the normalization denominators. Explain why the fermionic expression becomes singular as the two one-particle orbitals become identical.

Self-check: For identical spinless fermions, what is Ψ−(x,x) when the two orbital states are linearly independent?

Extension: combine a spin singlet with an antisymmetric spin triplet and examine how the spatial symmetry requirements change for electrons.

Scientific meaning

Conclusions, caveats and further reading

Exchange statistics constrain the wavefunction's symmetry, not simply classical particle-label counting. The interference of two orbital assignments produces distinct bosonic and fermionic position correlations. This is a spatial counterpart of bosonic enhancement and fermionic Fock-space signs.
