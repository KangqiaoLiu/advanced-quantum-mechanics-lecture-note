The same state can have different coordinates

Rotate the representation basis while keeping the physical preparation and observable fixed. See how the state's coordinates and the observable's matrix both change to preserve every expectation value.

Start with a physical question

What is the difference between rotating an apparatus and rotating its coordinates?

The column vector used to represent a quantum state depends on a choice of basis. A unitary change of basis changes those components, and also the matrix elements of every operator, while leaving the underlying physical probability unchanged. Confusing an active physical rotation with a passive change of coordinates leads to apparent paradoxes: one may change state coordinates but accidentally leave the operator matrix in the old basis.

Predict before touching a control. Prepare a |z+〉 state and measure σz. In a basis rotated by 90°, the state's first coordinate has probability 1/2. Should the actual σz measurement probability become 1/2 too?

Prerequisites. Two-dimensional normalized vectors, unitary matrices and expectation values 〈ψ|A|ψ〉. Read these explanations at your own pace; the experiment is designed for private self-study alongside the lecture notes.

Derive the prediction

Change the coordinate frame consistently

Let {|0〉,|1〉} be the original z basis and U=Ry(θ) a unitary whose columns define the rotated basis vectors. An abstract state |ψ〉 has original coefficients ψ and new coefficients ψ′=U†ψ. The same physical observable A has matrix A′=U†AU. Consequently

〈A〉 = ψ†Aψ = ψ′†A′ψ′.

For ψ=Ry(α)|0〉 and A=σz, the correct expectation is cosα. In the rotated coordinates, ψ′=Ry(α−θ)|0〉. The matrix A′=cosθσz−sinθσx is the representation of the original σz operator, which compensates exactly for the coordinate change:

〈A′〉=cosθcos(α−θ)−sinθsin(α−θ)=cosα.

By contrast, applying the old σz matrix to the new coefficients answers a different physical question: it effectively rotates the measured direction. This is an active change, and its expectation cos(α−θ) generally varies.

Check a limiting case directly

At θ=0, ψ′=ψ and A′=A. At θ=90° and α=0, the state has equal probability in the rotated basis while the correctly transformed observable still predicts a certain +z outcome. At θ=180°, the basis vectors are exchanged up to phases and the matrix A′ changes sign.

What assumptions define the calculation?

Passive global unitary basis transformations for a single qubit. The illustration uses real rotations about the y axis; complex unitary matrices work identically. All predictions refer to the same physical preparation and fixed original σz observable.

Explore → compare → understand

Three independent investigations

  1. Locate the apparent paradox. Start at α=0, vary θ. Watch the rotated-basis component probability change while the fixed physical σz expectation stays +1.
  2. Compare two questions. Fix α=65°, vary θ. Distinguish measuring σz in original laboratory coordinates from asking for the first-coordinate probability in a rotated basis.
  3. Verify covariance algebraically. Write U†σzU for θ=90° and multiply ψ′†A′ψ′ by hand. Verify that the number agrees with cosα.

Self-check: Under ψ′=U†ψ, which operator matrix gives the same physical observable in the new basis?

Interpretation and source links

What this experiment establishes

This example establishes the operational equivalence of representations. The same principle allows Schrödinger and Heisenberg pictures, changing Bloch bases and choosing different band eigenvector gauges without altering the appropriate measurable quantities.
