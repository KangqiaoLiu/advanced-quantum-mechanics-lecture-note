Start with a physical question

Can a complex matrix have entirely real energies?

A Hermitian quantum Hamiltonian may contain complex off-diagonal entries even though its eigenvalues must be real. A spin-½ Hamiltonian becomes especially transparent when written as a real vector multiplying the three Pauli matrices. Its energies depend on the vector length; its eigenstates depend on the vector direction. This separation lets us change phase conventions, locate a true degeneracy and interpret how eigenvectors rotate without solving a different matrix problem every time.

Predict before touching a control. For a fixed |g|, rotate the coupling phase from 0 to 180°. Predict whether the energy gap changes and whether the transverse spin expectation remains in the same direction.

Prerequisites. Pauli matrices, Hermitian conjugation, vector normalization and two-by-two eigenvalues. Read these explanations at your own pace; the experiment is designed for private self-study alongside the lecture notes.

How does the upper eigenstate change basis character?

Track the upper eigenstate probability of the σz=+1 basis outcome.

P(↑z|E+)=½(1+z/√[z²+g²]); at a true degeneracy the eigenvector is not unique, which is recorded explicitly.

Complex matrix phase

Eigenvector transverse expectation versus φ

The complex off-diagonal phase rotates the eigenstate on the equatorial plane.

〈σx〉=g cosφ/E and 〈σy〉=g sinφ/E. The phase-sensitive orientation is physical relative to the chosen fixed measurement axes.

Derive the prediction

A Hermitian matrix is a real Pauli vector

Introduce H=[[z,ge−iφ],[geiφ,−z]]. The off-diagonal matrix elements are complex conjugates, so H=H†. Write H=gcosφ σx+gsinφ σy+zσz. Pauli anticommutation yields H²=(g²+z²)I directly.

E±=±√(z²+g²),   ΔE=2√(z²+g²).

The normalized upper eigenstate has Bloch vector r+=(gcosφ,gsinφ,z)/√(z²+g²). A measurement of σz gives P(↑z)=(1+rz)/2. Its phase dependence is encoded in the transverse components. At g=z=0 the Hamiltonian is proportional to the identity and any orthonormal basis diagonalizes it; a unique eigenspinor cannot be assigned.

Because H is Hermitian, its eigenvectors can be chosen orthonormal and the spectral projectors P±=(I±H/E)/2 are positive rank-one projectors whenever E>0.

Check a limiting case directly

When g=0 and z>0, the upper eigenstate is |↑z〉. When z=0 and g>0, both σz outcomes have probability 1/2 and the upper eigenstate lies on the Bloch equator. When z=g=0, the gap vanishes and the labels upper/lower eigenvector become ambiguous.

What assumptions define the calculation?

One isolated two-state sector with no decay or non-Hermitian terms, energies in a common arbitrary unit, and a fixed {|↑z〉,|↓z〉} basis. The coupling phase is defined relative to measurement axes; basis-phase changes must transform both Hamiltonian and observables.

Explore → compare → understand

Three independent investigations

  1. Find the gap. Choose g=.65 and vary z through zero. Locate the minimum separation and compare it against 2g. Then set g=0 to recover a real level crossing.
  2. Rotate without changing energy. Fix z=.5, g=.9; vary φ. Confirm all energies remain constant while 〈σx〉 and 〈σy〉 trace trigonometric functions.
  3. Locate the undefined eigenstate. Set z=g=0. Explain which measurement probabilities can be assigned only after specifying a prepared state, since H no longer selects a unique eigenvector.

Self-check: For z=0, g=1 and any coupling phase, what is P(↑z) in the upper energy eigenstate?

Interpretation and source links

What this experiment establishes

The Pauli-vector representation simultaneously explains Hermitian eigenvalue reality, eigenstate geometry and degeneracies. It forms a reusable mathematical bridge to magnetic spin precession, Dirac spinors, avoided level crossings, and two-band topology.
