Can different preparations be the same quantum state?

Compare two independently prepared statistical ensembles, compute their Bloch vectors and density operators, and ask whether any measurement can distinguish them.

Question and first prediction

What does it mean for two different ensembles to be experimentally indistinguishable?

A density operator represents all statistics accessible from repeated measurements on identically prepared systems. The same operator can arise from many different classical randomizations over pure states. A fair mixture of |z+〉 and |z−〉 is identical to a fair mixture of |x+〉 and |x−〉, even though the underlying preparation instructions are very different. By allowing the mixture weights to differ, we can observe exactly when the two density operators become distinguishable by a suitable measurement.

Predict before exploring. Prepare ensemble A from 50% |z+〉 and 50% |z−〉, and ensemble B from 50% |x+〉 and 50% |x−〉. Predict all σz and σx measurement probabilities and decide whether a measurement on the qubit can reveal which ensemble procedure was used.

Recommended background: Pure-state projectors, classical preparation probabilities, Bloch vectors and the trace of a product of operators.

What the same detector can distinguish

A single projective measurement axis can produce different outcomes for ensembles whose density operators differ.

P₊(θ)=[1+r·n(θ)]/2. Ensemble A has r=(0,0,2p−1), while ensemble B has r=(2q−1,0,0). Identical density operators predict identical results for every axis.

Statistical distinguishability

Trace distance between the two density operators

The optimal single-shot measurement distinguishing two equiprobable density operators is determined by their difference.

D(ρA,ρB)=½|rA−rB|. For equal priors, the optimal correct discrimination probability is (1+D)/2. The coincidence at p=q=1/2 is exact.

Mathematical explanation

Classical mixing produces a quantum density operator

Define ensembles A and B by ρA=p|z+〉〈z+|+(1−p)|z−〉〈z−| and ρB=q|x+〉〈x+|+(1−q)|x−〉〈x−|. Using Pauli matrices and the identity |n+〉〈n+|=(I+n·σ)/2 gives

ρA=½[I+(2p−1)σz],   ρB=½[I+(2q−1)σx].

At p=q=1/2, both reduce exactly to I/2. Although the list of pure states in the two preparation recipes differs, every observable A has the same expectation Tr(ρA A)=Tr(ρB A). No qubit-only measurement can reveal the particular classical decomposition.

For a Bloch vector r, purity is Tr(ρ²)=(1+|r|²)/2, and the binary von Neumann entropy is h₂[(1+|r|)/2], where h₂(x)=−xlog₂x−(1−x)log₂(1−x). The trace distance between two qubit density operators is half the Euclidean length of their Bloch-vector difference.

Test an exactly solvable limit

At p=q=1/2 both ensembles are maximally mixed, have entropy one bit and trace distance zero. At p=1 and q=1 they are different pure states |z+〉 and |x+〉 with trace distance 1/√2. When the measurement axis points along z, ensemble B's + probability remains 1/2 for every q.

Specify the model's validity

Independent classical random preparation of qubit pure states with exact ensemble weights, no retained classical label available to the observer, and an ideal two-outcome Pauli measurement. Access to the preparation label would change the operational scenario and permit distinctions not present in the qubit density alone.

Follow a self-directed learning path

Three quantitative investigations

  1. Show equivalence. Set p=q=.5 and rotate the measurement axis. Confirm the curves overlap and both purities equal 1/2. Explain why the decomposition is experimentally irrelevant.
  2. Find the most distinguishing axis. Set p=.8, q=.3 and sweep θ. Compare the probability difference against the vector rA−rB and identify which basis would maximize it.
  3. Check state versus procedure. Choose pure states p=q=1. Decide which information belongs to the prepared density operator and which belongs only to the classical preparation log.

Check your understanding: Which statement is true of the equally weighted ±z and ±x ensembles when only the qubit is accessible?

Extension: derive the optimal Helstrom measurement direction by diagonalizing ρA−ρB.

Connect back to quantum theory

What you can conclude

The density operator describes the operational quantum state, while a pure-state ensemble decomposition is generally nonunique. This conceptual distinction is foundational for quantum tomography, thermodynamics, open systems and understanding entanglement versus classical uncertainty.
