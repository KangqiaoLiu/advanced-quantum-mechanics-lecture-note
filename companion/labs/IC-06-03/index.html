Why does a bosonic ladder operator contain a square root?

Build number states in two bosonic modes, apply creation and annihilation operators, and compare number states with Poissonian coherent occupations.

Physical phenomenon

Why does adding a boson to an occupied mode give a larger matrix element?

Indistinguishable bosons admit arbitrary occupation of the same orbital. A normalized many-body occupation basis absorbs factorial counting, so bosonic creation and annihilation coefficients grow as the square root of the occupation number. This algebra is the same for cold atoms in one orbital and for photons in one electromagnetic mode. A coherent state illustrates a distribution over Fock occupations with Poisson probabilities, providing a bridge to the oscillator experiment.

Make a prediction. Compare creating one particle in an empty orbital and in an orbital already containing four bosons. Predict the two amplitudes, and verify which operator ordering produces the canonical commutator.

Prerequisites: Symmetric wavefunctions, canonical commutation [b,b†]=1, occupation-number basis and harmonic-oscillator ladder operators.

Operator normalization

Square-root matrix elements

Changing occupation number changes the strength of a creation or annihilation process in the normalized Fock basis.

Creation factor √(n+1), annihilation factor √n. The y-axis is a matrix-element magnitude, not a transition probability; physical probabilities depend on the full interaction and duration.

Coherent-state occupation

From fixed number to a Poisson distribution

A coherent state is a normalized superposition of number states with amplitudes proportional to αⁿ/√(n!).

P(n)=exp(−|α|²)|α|²ⁿ/n!. A tail beyond n=20 is reported to distinguish exact Poisson weights from displayed truncation.

Reconstruct the result

Normalized symmetric states fix the ladder factors

For bosons, [bᵢ,b†ⱼ]=δᵢⱼ and all creation operators commute with one another. A normalized Fock state is |n₀,n₁〉=(b†₀)n₀(b†₁)n₁|vac〉/√(n₀!n₁!). Commuting an annihilation operator through n identical creation operators gives n equal terms.

bⱼ|…,nⱼ,…〉=√nⱼ|…,nⱼ−1,…〉,   b†ⱼ|…,nⱼ,…〉=√(nⱼ+1)|…,nⱼ+1,…〉.

Apply the operators in opposite orders: bb†|n〉=(n+1)|n〉 and b†b|n〉=n|n〉. Their difference gives the original state for every n. Distinct-mode operators commute independently. The number operator N=b†b has integer eigenvalues n≥0.

The coherent state |α〉=exp(−|α|²/2)Σₙ αⁿ|n〉/√(n!) is an eigenstate of b. Its measured occupation probabilities are Poissonian, with mean and variance |α|². The histogram is a second view of the same bosonic algebra rather than an assumption about thermal states.

An exactly soluble reference limit

For vacuum n=0, b|0〉=0 while b†|0〉=|1〉. For n=1, b†|1〉=√2|2〉. At α=0 the coherent distribution collapses entirely to n=0. At large |α|² its Poisson distribution spreads to multiple Fock occupations.

Assumptions and numerical reliability

Exact algebra in an ideal two-mode bosonic Fock space; mode occupation sliders select basis states independently. The coherent-state histogram is a separate one-mode example and is not implied by the selected two-mode occupation state. Physical optical/atomic transitions require specifying a Hamiltonian in addition to a ladder-operator matrix element.

Independent investigations

Three experiments to reason through

  1. Verify the commutator. At n=0,1,4 compare bb† and b†b acting on the same state. Explain why their squared ladder factors differ by exactly one.
  2. Compare Bose and Fermi rules. Set n=4 and create another boson. Contrast this √5 amplitude with fermionic Pauli blocking in the previous many-body experiment.
  3. Predict coherent fluctuations. Vary |α| and inspect how mean occupation and variance change. Check the probability sum and the unshown tail at large displacement.

Self-check: What is the coefficient of b†|4〉 in the normalized Fock basis?

Extension: derive the bosonic coherent-state Poisson distribution by taking squared magnitudes of its Fock amplitudes.

Physical scope

What the experiment means

Bose enhancement follows directly from symmetric many-particle normalization and commutation relations. Number states and coherent states organize the same Hilbert space in different ways. The next stages extend these rules to Hamiltonians and interactions in second quantization.
